Technical report

A simulation of United Airlines, with its behavior measured from history

What the simulator does, what was measured to build it, how it is checked against reality, and where it fails.

Summary

This is a simulation of United Airlines under changes in fuel, demand and aircraft deliveries, with the airline's response measured from history, not assumed. Two measurements held up: the price United pays follows spot fuel about days behind, and a year after a fuel cost change unit revenue has made up of it ( range ). Four did not: the recovery curve specified in advance lost its held-back test, no capacity response could be told from zero, airport traveler counts barely track United's traffic, and the best trading rule lost money on later years. All are co-movements, not causal estimates. The simulator is checked by replaying past quarters, where it only ties United's own guidance midpoint, and by an estimate for United's next quarter, fixed before the result is known.

The simulator

The simulator steps United's business forward month by month; the figure below shows its inputs and outputs. Each output is a range: the middle of many random runs, with a tenth of them beyond each bound.

Each scenario runs three times on the same random paths, changing only the behavior: no fare response, management's stated path, and the response measured from history. The application keeps the three questions of the Stage 1 proposal: what if, what breaks, and what must be true.

Data

Everything is public. Each raw download is stored with its hash, and every output lists its inputs. The years from 2024 were set aside as the test; the text says where a choice or a fit later used them.

Fuel cost to revenue

A recovery curve is the share of a fuel cost change that unit revenue has made up, quarter by quarter.

The curve specified in advance lost its test: on held-back quarters it missed by more than management's stated share used as a fixed rule, and more than full recovery at once, though the gaps are too small to tell from chance.

The curve now used has two parameters, the long-run share and the speed of reaching it. It was chosen after the test years were seen, so it has no clean test. Its share after one year, ( range ), is the best-determined number here; the speed is not determined.

Method, the speed, and industry fares

We regressed the yearly change in TRASM, total revenue per available seat mile, on the yearly change in fuel cost per seat mile in the same quarter and the four before, holding capacity growth fixed. The curve is the running sum of the coefficients. The two-parameter curve is fitted on all quarters. Its share after one year is in the middle of the resampled draws and on the single best fit. Does it match reality

Replay of past quarters

The full simulator cannot be rerun in past years, so the replay uses its chain: United's guidance midpoint, less the fuel cost that arrived after guidance and that the recovery curve says does not come back within the quarter. On the guided quarters from it missed reported earnings by a share on average, the same as the midpoint itself and as a rule with no recovery: a three-way tie. The method did not beat United's own number. Its range held of results, but a midpoint given the same range scores no worse, and its gap to the midpoint did not predict the share move on results day.

The fuel step is the one clear positive result: at quarter end the chain's fuel price was on average a gallon from the price United later reported; United's own stated price was away.

How the chain was chosen, and the results-day test

Of variants of the chain, the one used treats fuel that moved after the middle of the quarter as not recovered in that quarter. It was chosen on the guided quarters before 2024, too few to tell close variants apart. The fuel comparison covers the quarters for which United stated a price, of them before 2024.

On the held-back quarters, each dollar of gap between the chain's estimate and the midpoint went with a move of in United's shares against the market over the results day and the next. The range () includes zero, and gap and move had the same sign in of the quarters.

Width of the ranges

The simulator's ranges were checked for width. With the assumed demand noise, the range held of unit-revenue outcomes in 2022 to 2026 () and of operating-margin outcomes (). It held every outcome before 2020, so it is too wide for calm markets. A value fitted on those years held only later and was set aside.

How the check works, and what sets the width

Each outcome is the miss of a naive forecast made two, three or four quarters earlier, set against the gap between two simulated worlds at the same distance. The outcomes come from quarters and overlap, so the counts are not independent. The assumed demand noise is a month and the fitted value ; the assumed one was kept because of this coverage, so the figure is the reason for the choice, not a separate test of it.

What would count as being wrong

A single miss settles nothing: one result in five should fall outside the range. The measured response is wrong if over several quarters it scores worse than the guidance midpoint or results keep landing outside its range. So far it only ties the midpoint, and the fuel-bill range already fails the second test.

The agent

An analyst in the application plans, runs tools and writes. The language model never types a number: tool results reach it as labeled facts, it writes placeholders, and code puts the values in.

A second component reads guidance from United's filings and verifies each quoted value against the document: 159 documents gave 509 verified facts.

What happens when a check fails

The tools look up results, run the simulator and do arithmetic. A report with a typed digit is rejected. After one rewrite it is assembled from the tool results without the model. When no model can be reached, a rules planner handles the common kinds of question. The check cannot catch a figure placed beside the wrong words, so each figure carries its label.

For guidance, code finds candidate passages, a picker (fixed rules or the model) chooses one or abstains, code parses the value, and the verifier compares the quote with the document. Every fact in the file was picked by the rules.

Limits

  • Small samples. The recovery curve rests on quarters, and its ranges assume the model is right.
  • Co-movement, not cause. Fuel and travel demand move with the economy, so part of every measured response is demand.
  • Few shocks. The fuel move of 2026 is larger than any the curve was first fitted on.
  • Symmetry is assumed. A fall in fuel is treated as the mirror of a rise, and the one test had little power.
  • Range width comes mostly from fuel volatility over the trading days before the cutoff, times that of 2013 to 2019.
  • The simulator has no seasonality.
  • The cash model is simplified. Advance ticket sales, debt service and profit sharing are not modeled.
  • Shares of runs are not probabilities. They describe the model, not the chance of an event.

Scaling

Adding an airline takes its quarterly figures, its guidance, and a note of where its accounts differ. Delta was the trial: the same code fetched and indexed its filings, but its results needed their own reader, and only its adjusted figures compare with United's. Daily fares beyond 2022 and bookings, neither public, would improve it most.
